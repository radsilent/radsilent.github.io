About

I'm Matthew Collaro, a systems engineer and software developer. My work sits at the seam where engineering programs usually break: between the model and the specification, between the specification and the test, and between the test and the evidence that the test actually proved something.

Most of what I build is tooling for that problem — software that keeps requirements, architecture decisions, and verification results linked to each other as a program changes, instead of letting them drift apart over months until nobody can say which requirement a given design decision was meant to satisfy.

The through-line in my work is a conviction that traceability shouldn't be a documentation task. On most programs it's treated as one: engineers do the real work in their models and their test benches, and then somebody reconstructs the links afterward, from memory, under deadline. That reconstruction is where the errors get in, and it's why a requirement can sit unsatisfied for two review cycles before anyone notices.

I'd rather the links be a side effect of the engineering itself — captured when the decision is made, checked continuously, and surfaced the moment they break. That's the problem I've spent my time on, and most of what I've built is some attempt at it.

What I work on

Model-based systems engineering tooling

Requirements management on large programs is still largely a document problem pretending to be an engineering problem. A requirement gets written in one tool, modeled in a second, tested in a third, and the links between those three are maintained by hand in a spreadsheet — until they aren't.

I build software that treats those links as first-class data: a governed graph where a change to a requirement propagates to the architecture elements that implement it and the verification artifacts that close it out, so the traceability is a byproduct of doing the work rather than a separate documentation exercise performed after the fact.

Continuous verification

Verification is usually treated as a phase — something that happens near the end, against a baseline that has already moved. I'm interested in the alternative: verification that runs continuously against the current state of the design, so that a broken trace or an unsatisfied requirement surfaces the day it breaks rather than at a review months later.
