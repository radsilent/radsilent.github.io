Continuous Verification for System Models

Software solved this thirty years ago. Systems engineering hasn't adopted the lesson.

Software engineering went through this transition already. There was a time when integration was a phase — you developed in isolation for months, then entered an integration period whose duration nobody could predict, because the defects you were about to discover had been accumulating invisibly the entire time. Continuous integration didn't make those defects less likely. It made them surface within minutes of introduction, when the person who caused them still remembered what they were doing.

Systems engineering has not made this transition. Verification is still a phase. It runs against a baseline, the baseline is established at a milestone, and by the time verification results come back, the design has moved. We are still doing big-bang integration, and we are still surprised by the results.

What "continuous" can and cannot mean here

An immediate objection: you can't continuously verify a physical system. Qualification testing takes weeks, environmental testing requires hardware that doesn't exist yet, and no amount of CI enthusiasm changes the lead time on a test article. That objection is correct, and any honest proposal has to concede it.

But it conflates two things. Verification that a system meets its requirements does require the system. Verification that the engineering artifacts are internally consistent requires only the artifacts — and that second category is where a startling share of program defects actually originate. Not "the actuator failed to meet its response time," but "the response time requirement was allocated to two components with contradictory budgets, and nobody noticed for five months."

That second class is fully checkable, today, without hardware, in seconds. It is currently checked by humans reading documents at scheduled reviews.

The checks worth running

Most of what's valuable is unglamorous. In rough order of return on effort:

Structural completeness. Every leaf requirement has at least one satisfying element. Every satisfying element exists in the current model. Every requirement with a satisfy relationship also has a verify relationship. Every interface has both a provider and a consumer. These are graph queries. They take milliseconds and they catch the gaps that otherwise surface at a design review, where the cost of discovery is a room full of senior engineers.

Budget conservation. Where a top-level quantity is decomposed across subsystems — mass, power, latency, bandwidth, thermal dissipation — the children should sum within the parent's allocation. This is arithmetic. It is almost never automated, and it is very often violated, because each subsystem owner negotiates their allocation independently and no single person recomputes the total after every negotiation.

Interface consistency. A signal produced by one component and consumed by another should agree on type, units, range, and update rate at both ends. Unit mismatches in particular are both notorious and trivially detectable, provided units are modeled as data rather than written into a description field as prose.

Staleness. When a requirement's text changes, every trace link touching it becomes suspect until re-reviewed. This one is often resisted, because it generates work. That resistance is worth examining honestly: the work exists either way. The only question is whether it's done incrementally as changes occur, or discovered in bulk at a milestone.

Why it isn't already standard

The technical barrier is real but not the main one. To run any of these checks automatically, the model has to be machine-readable, the requirements have to be machine-readable, the links between them have to be typed, and all three have to live somewhere a pipeline can reach without a human exporting anything. On a lot of programs at least one of those is false — the requirements are in a database that exports to Word, or the model lives in a repository behind a desktop client with no usable API, or the trace links exist only in a spreadsheet.

So the first real work is plumbing, and plumbing is hard to fund because it produces no visible artifact. There is no slide that shows the value of an ingestion pipeline.

The second barrier is cultural, and larger. Continuous verification produces a continuous stream of findings, and on a program where reviews are formal events with formal outcomes, a stream of findings is uncomfortable. It makes visible a defect rate that was always present but previously only sampled at milestones. I've seen teams react to the first run of an automated consistency check by questioning the check, which is the natural response to being told your model has four hundred inconsistencies when the last review found six.

The four hundred were always there. The review found six because a human read a subset of the documents for a few hours. That is the actual comparison, and it's worth stating plainly when the objection comes up.

Making the output survivable

Which raises the practical design problem: a check that reports four hundred findings on first run will be switched off. Any deployment has to handle this deliberately.

The approach that works is the one static analysis tools converged on — baseline the existing violations, and gate only on new ones. The program starts with a known debt, the debt is visible and quantified, and the pipeline's job is to prevent it from growing. Paying it down becomes a separate, schedulable activity rather than a precondition for turning the tooling on at all.

It also matters enormously that findings are precise. "Model has inconsistencies" is noise. "Requirement PWR-114 allocates 40 W to the sensor module; the module's three children allocate 46 W total; the discrepancy was introduced in revision 87 when SENS-3's allocation increased from 8 W to 14 W" is actionable, and it's the kind of output a versioned graph can produce and a document export cannot.

What changes

When this works, the milestone review stops being the moment defects are discovered and becomes the moment decisions are made — which is what it was always supposed to be. The consistency questions have already been answered by machines. The humans in the room get to spend their time on the judgment calls that actually require them.

That's the argument. It isn't that automated checking is clever. It's that we are currently using some of the most expensive engineering hours available to perform arithmetic and cross-referencing, at a rate of once per quarter, and calling the result assurance.
