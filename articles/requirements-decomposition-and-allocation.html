Decomposition Is Where Requirements Go Wrong

Requirements quality effort goes into authoring. The defects come from the level below.

There is a large literature on writing good requirements. Use "shall." Avoid ambiguous adjectives. Make it verifiable, atomic, unambiguous, necessary. Requirements training is mostly training in authoring, and review checklists are mostly authoring checklists.

That effort is not misplaced, but it targets the wrong stage. In my experience the defects that actually hurt programs are not badly written requirements. They're well-written requirements that were decomposed incorrectly — and decomposition receives a fraction of the scrutiny, because the children individually pass every authoring check.

The sufficiency problem

When a parent requirement is decomposed into children, an obligation is created that almost nothing checks: the children, collectively, must be sufficient to satisfy the parent. Satisfying every child must imply satisfying the parent.

This is not verified on most programs. What is verified is that every child traces to a parent — the direction that's easy to check, since it's a property of each individual link. Sufficiency is a property of the set, and sets are harder. You cannot look at one child and tell whether the group is complete. You have to reason about whether anything is missing, and "is anything missing" is a judgment that no completeness metric captures.

So the common failure is a parent requirement decomposed into four children that are each individually correct, each traceable, each verifiable — and that collectively miss a case. All four children pass verification. The parent is marked satisfied by inference. The gap surfaces during integration, or in the field.

The mitigation isn't automation, because sufficiency isn't computable in general. It's making the obligation explicit at decomposition time: recording, alongside the decomposition, the argument for why these children are sufficient. Not a formal proof — a sentence or two of rationale that a reviewer can attack. Most decompositions have such an argument in the author's head. Almost none write it down, and once it isn't written down there is nothing for review to engage with, so review defaults to checking each child in isolation, which is exactly the check that cannot find the defect.

Budgets and the negotiation problem

Quantitative decomposition has a cleaner failure mode and a more tractable fix. When a top-level budget — mass, power, latency, bandwidth, thermal — is allocated across subsystems, the children should sum within the parent.

This is arithmetic, and it is violated constantly. The reason is organizational rather than mathematical. Allocations are negotiated pairwise: a subsystem owner argues they need more, the systems engineer agrees, the allocation is adjusted. That conversation happens between two people and is recorded in the requirement for that subsystem. What is not recomputed is the total, because nobody's job is to recompute the total after each pairwise negotiation, and there may be dozens over a design phase.

The result is a budget that was balanced at baseline and drifted into overrun through a series of individually reasonable decisions, each of which appeared affordable in isolation. The overrun surfaces when someone finally sums the column, usually late, usually at a point where recovering it means design changes across several subsystems.

This one is fully automatable, and it's the highest-return check I know of in requirements management. If allocations are structured data rather than numbers embedded in requirement prose, conservation is a query that runs on every change. The obstacle is almost always that the allocation lives inside a sentence — "The sensor module shall consume no more than 14 W" — where it's invisible to any tool. Extracting quantities into typed attributes is unglamorous data modeling that pays for itself the first time it catches a drift.

Derived requirements and lost rationale

A derived requirement doesn't decompose from a parent; it arises from a design decision. Choosing a particular bus architecture creates timing requirements that no stakeholder asked for and that exist only because of that choice.

These are legitimate and unavoidable. The problem is that they get recorded as requirements without recording what they derive from, and then they outlive the decision that produced them. The architecture changes. The derived requirement stays, because nothing links it to the now-obsolete choice. Years later it's a constraint nobody can explain, and it gets treated as a given, because the safe response to a requirement you don't understand is to comply with it.

Every mature program accumulates these. I've watched engineers design around a constraint that existed because of a component selected two revisions earlier and no longer in the system. The fix is cheap and structural: derived requirements should link to the decision that created them, not just to a parent requirement. Then when the decision is revisited, what it created is enumerable.

Allocation to the wrong kind of thing

A subtler failure: allocating a requirement to an organizational unit rather than to an architectural element. "The avionics team shall ensure..." — which feels natural, because allocation often happens in the same conversation as work assignment.

It breaks traceability in a specific way. Teams reorganize. Scope moves. When the team is dissolved or renamed, the allocation points at nothing, and the requirement is orphaned without any structural signal that it has been. Allocating to elements means the link survives reorganization, and responsibility is derived from element ownership — a mapping that can change without touching the requirement at all.

What review should ask

If I were rewriting a decomposition review checklist, it would ask about the set rather than the items. Why are these children sufficient? What case would fail if all of them passed? Do the quantitative allocations sum within the parent, and when was that last computed? Which of these are derived rather than decomposed, and what decision produced them? Is each allocated to an element that will still exist after the next reorganization?

None of these appear on a standard checklist, and every one of them targets a defect class that authoring checks are structurally incapable of finding.
