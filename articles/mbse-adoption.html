Why MBSE Adoption Stalls

The obstacles are almost never the ones in the rollout plan.

I've spent a good part of my career on MBSE adoption inside large engineering organizations — writing the governance, building the tooling, and running a community of practice for a group of engineers large enough that I could watch adoption succeed in some corners and quietly die in others. The pattern in the failures was consistent, and it had very little to do with modeling.

The mandate problem

Most rollouts begin with a mandate. Leadership decides the organization will be model-based, a directive follows, and teams are told to model.

What this produces is compliance, which resembles adoption from a distance and is a fundamentally different thing. A team under a mandate builds a model because it's required, sized to satisfy the requirement and no larger. The model is built once, near the milestone that demands it, and then abandoned, because nothing downstream depends on it. It is a deliverable, and after delivery it has no function.

The tell is easy to check: ask when a model was last opened by someone who wasn't updating it for a review. On compliance-driven programs the answer is never. The model has no readers. And a model with no readers is pure cost — real effort producing an artifact that informs no decision.

Adoption follows dependency

Where I've seen it stick, it stuck because something the team needed came out of the model and could not be gotten any other way.

A generated interface document that's always current, so nobody maintains tables by hand. An automated consistency check that catches the class of defect that burned the team last program. A safety analysis that reads the architecture directly instead of requiring someone to transcribe it. In each case the team maintains the model because they consume something from it, and letting it go stale costs them personally and immediately.

That's the actual mechanism: adoption follows dependency, not directive. Which reframes the rollout question from "how do we get teams to model" to "what can we generate from a model that teams currently produce painfully by hand." The second question has concrete answers and each one is a wedge.

The transcription trap

A specific and very common failure: the model is built by someone other than the engineers who own the design.

It happens for understandable reasons. Modeling tools have a learning curve, the organization has a few skilled modelers, and the efficient-looking move is to have them build the models by interviewing subsystem owners. The result is a transcription. The modeler captures their understanding of what the engineer said, at one point in time. The engineer doesn't own it, doesn't read it, and doesn't update it, because it isn't theirs and it isn't where they work. It's stale within a month.

The models that stay alive are built by the people who own the design. This means accepting lower modeling quality in exchange for currency and ownership — a trade experienced modelers resist, because the resulting models violate conventions and use stereotypes inconsistently. But a rough model the owner maintains is worth more than an elegant one nobody touches, and the conventions can be tightened later, once the habit exists.

Tooling friction compounds

Modeling tools are, generally, not pleasant. Heavy clients, slow load times, awkward repository operations, merge conflicts resolved through interfaces that make you guess. An engineer who has to wait two minutes for a model to open, navigate several dialogs to make a small edit, and then survive a fragile check-in will batch their edits. And batching means the model reflects the design as of the last batch, not as of now.

This friction is usually treated as a fixed cost of the tool. It shouldn't be. Some of it is genuinely fixed, but a surprising amount is configuration, repository structure, and workflow — model partitioning that reduces conflicts, sensible branching, scripted operations for the common tedious tasks. Time spent reducing per-edit cost has, in my experience, a higher return than any amount of training, because it changes the economics of the behavior you want rather than exhorting people toward it.

The measurement problem

Adoption programs need to report progress, and the available metrics are bad. Number of models. Number of elements. Percentage of subsystems with a model. Engineers trained.

All of these are inputs, and all of them are gameable — trivially so. Element count in particular rewards bloat. I've seen models grow enormous under measurement while becoming less useful, because the incentive was volume.

Better metrics measure consumption rather than production. How many artifacts are generated from models rather than authored by hand. How many automated checks run against models per week. How many engineers opened a model they don't own. These are harder to collect and they measure the thing you actually want — whether the model has become load-bearing.

Communities of practice, honestly assessed

I've run one, and I'd say their value is real but different from what's usually claimed. They're not primarily a training mechanism; a community meeting is a poor way to teach a tool. What they do well is make practice visible across organizational boundaries — a team discovers another team solved the problem they're stuck on, patterns propagate without anyone standardizing them, and the person who built something useful gets an audience for it.

They also fail in a recognizable way: becoming a status meeting where each team reports progress to leadership. Once that happens, attendance becomes obligation and the cross-pollination stops. Keeping it a peer forum rather than a reporting line is the main thing worth protecting.

The honest summary

If I had to compress it: don't mandate modeling, build something teams want that requires a model as input. Let the design owners build their own models even when the models are worse. Spend real effort on per-edit friction. Measure consumption, not production. And expect the whole thing to take longer than the plan says, because you're not deploying a tool — you're changing where a large group of engineers believe the authoritative description of the system lives, and that belief moves slowly and only in response to evidence.
